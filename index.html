Accidental ingestion

Act quickly. If someone may have swallowed medicine, a chemical, poison, or an unknown substance, contact your local poison information service or emergency services promptly—even if they feel well.

What to do

  • Check responsiveness and breathing. If they collapse, have a seizure, struggle to breathe, or cannot be awakened, call emergency services immediately.
  • Spit out any remaining substance. Gently wipe/rinse the mouth; have them spit the rinse out.
  • Keep the product container, label, or a photo. Note the substance, estimated amount, and time swallowed.
  • Follow poison-specialist or emergency clinician instructions. If the person is unconscious or not fully alert, give nothing by mouth.

What NOT to do

  • Do not make them vomit or use ipecac.
  • Do not give food, drinks, milk, oil, alcohol, or home remedies unless a poison specialist or clinician specifically instructs you.
  • Do not try to neutralize it with vinegar, lemon, baking soda, or another chemical.
  • Do not give activated charcoal unless directed by a poison specialist or clinician.
  • Do not wait for symptoms or leave the person alone.

Important: Do not put yourself at risk. If a chemical is also on the skin or in the eyes, remove contaminated clothing and rinse with running water while seeking expert help. For inhaled fumes, move to fresh air only if safe.

Danger signs — get emergency help

Call your local emergency number immediately if a person has any of these warning signs:

  • Not breathing normally, gasping, or severe difficulty breathing.
  • Unresponsive, suddenly confused, or difficult to wake.
  • Chest pain or pressure, especially with sweating, nausea, or shortness of breath.
  • Stroke signs: face drooping, one arm weak, or sudden speech difficulty. Note when symptoms began.
  • Seizure, especially a first, prolonged, or repeated seizure.
  • Severe bleeding, major injury, or signs of shock such as pale/cold/clammy skin, weakness, or fainting.
  • Severe allergic reaction with trouble breathing, throat/tongue swelling, or collapse.
  • Sudden severe headache, or sudden loss of vision, balance, or strength.

While waiting for help

  • Stay with the person, keep the scene safe, and follow the dispatcher’s instructions.
  • If unresponsive and not breathing normally, start CPR and use an AED if available; follow dispatcher coaching.
  • Do not give food, drink, or medication to someone who is drowsy or unconscious.

Remember: This guide cannot diagnose an emergency. If you are unsure and the person seems seriously unwell, seek emergency help.

Baby danger signs — get emergency help

For a baby under 1 year: Call emergency services immediately if you notice any of these warning signs.

  • Not breathing, gasping, pauses in breathing, or severe difficulty breathing; grunting, ribs pulling in, or blue/gray lips or face.
  • Unresponsive, unusually floppy, very hard to wake, or not responding normally.
  • Seizure, especially a first seizure, prolonged seizure, or repeated seizures.
  • Choking or unable to cry, cough, or breathe.
  • Sudden pale, blue, gray, or mottled skin; very cold and weak, or signs of shock.
  • Severe bleeding, major injury, or a serious burn.

Urgent medical assessment

  • Baby younger than 3 months with a temperature of 38°C (100.4°F) or higher.
  • Unable to feed, repeated vomiting, markedly fewer wet diapers, or other signs of dehydration.
  • Unusual weak/high-pitched cry, bulging soft spot when calm and upright, or rapidly worsening condition.

While waiting: Stay with the baby, keep them comfortably warm, and follow the emergency dispatcher’s instructions. If they are unresponsive and not breathing normally, start infant CPR if you know how or follow dispatcher coaching. Do not shake the baby or give food, drink, or medicine to an unresponsive baby.

This is not a diagnosis. When concerned, seek urgent professional medical advice; babies can become seriously ill quickly.

Baby emergency signs: feeding, breathing, crying, color & vomiting

Call emergency services now if a baby stops breathing, is gasping or struggling to breathe, turns blue/gray, becomes unresponsive or very floppy, or may have inhaled vomit and is having breathing trouble. Put the phone on speaker and follow dispatcher instructions.

What to do

  • Not feeding: If baby is alert and breathing normally, do not force-feed. Seek urgent medical advice if refusing feeds, especially with fewer wet diapers, fever, repeated vomiting, unusual sleepiness, or worsening condition. A baby under 3 months with a temperature of 38°C / 100.4°F or higher needs urgent medical assessment.
  • Stops breathing / not breathing normally: Call emergency services. If unresponsive and not breathing normally, begin infant CPR immediately if trained, or follow dispatcher coaching. Use an AED as soon as available, following its prompts; do not delay CPR to search for one.
  • Inconsolable crying: Check breathing, color, temperature, injury, and whether something is constricting fingers/toes. If crying is sudden, unusual, persistent, or accompanied by fever, vomiting, lethargy, breathing difficulty, or color change, seek urgent medical care. Never shake a baby.
  • Pale, blue/gray, or mottled (blotchy) color: If sudden, persistent, or paired with breathing problems, weakness, cold skin, or reduced responsiveness, call emergency services. Keep baby comfortably warm and monitor breathing.
  • Vomiting / possible aspiration: If baby is awake and breathing, hold them upright or turn their head to the side and gently clear visible vomit from the mouth. Do not sweep blindly inside the mouth. If unresponsive but breathing normally, place on their side if safe and monitor breathing. If not breathing normally, start CPR and follow dispatcher instructions.

What NOT to do

  • Do not force milk, water, food, or medicine into a distressed, sleepy, choking, or breathing-impaired baby.
  • Do not put fingers or objects into the mouth unless you can clearly see and safely remove an obstruction.
  • Do not hang the baby upside down, shake them, slap their back for ordinary vomiting, or try to make them vomit.
  • Do not leave the baby alone, assume mottling or color change is harmless, or delay emergency help to take photos/videos.
  • Do not give infant CPR breaths or chest compressions based on this text alone if unsure; call emergency services and follow live dispatcher guidance.

Reminder: This guide cannot assess an infant remotely. If you are worried or symptoms are worsening, get professional medical help promptly.

Animal bites & stings

For any bite or sting, move away from the animal safely. Call emergency services for trouble breathing, facial/throat swelling, collapse, severe bleeding, seizure, or rapidly worsening symptoms. Wash wounds and get prompt medical assessment—rabies and tetanus prevention may be time-sensitive.

Dog or cat bite — ask these questions

  • Was it a domestic/pet animal or a stray/unknown animal? Can the animal and its owner be safely identified?
  • Is the animal’s rabies vaccination known and current? Can a public-health or veterinary authority arrange safe observation?
  • When and where did the bite happen? Did it break the skin, and where on the body?
  • Do not try to catch or handle a stray or aggressive animal. Report it to local animal-control/public-health authorities.

Wash a bite or scratch thoroughly with soap and running water for about 15 minutes, if possible. Apply pressure for bleeding and cover with a clean dressing. Seek medical care promptly for rabies-risk assessment, infection prevention, and tetanus advice. Do not wait for symptoms or for the animal to be found before seeking advice.

Snake bite

  • Move away from the snake; do not try to catch, kill, or handle it. Note its appearance from a safe distance only if easy.
  • Keep the person calm and still; immobilize the bitten limb in a comfortable position and remove rings or tight items.
  • Call emergency services and get urgent hospital care for possible antivenom assessment.
  • Do not cut, suck, squeeze, ice, burn, or apply a tight tourniquet or pressure bandage unless specifically directed by local expert protocols.

Spider or scorpion sting

  • Move away from the creature. Wash the area with soap and water; use a cool pack wrapped in cloth for brief periods.
  • Seek urgent medical or poison-service advice, especially for children, severe pain, spreading symptoms, sweating, vomiting, muscle cramps, abnormal movements, or a suspected dangerous species.
  • Call emergency services for breathing difficulty, collapse, seizures, severe whole-body symptoms, or facial/throat swelling.
  • Do not cut or suck the site, apply a tourniquet, or use unverified remedies. Do not delay care to capture the creature.

Management & follow-up advice

  • Dog/cat bites: Irrigate and clean thoroughly, control bleeding with direct pressure, and cover loosely. A clinician should assess rabies exposure, tetanus status, wound depth, and whether antibiotics or wound closure are appropriate. Bites to the face, hand, joints, genitals, deep punctures, or wounds in immunocompromised people need especially prompt assessment. If the animal is a pet, obtain owner/vaccination details without delaying care; public-health authorities advise on observation and rabies prevention.
  • Snake bites: Arrange urgent transport to a hospital; keep the person still and the limb supported. Do not let them walk if avoidable. Mark swelling edge and time if this can be done without delaying transport. Hospital teams determine monitoring and antivenom treatment. Do not use tight pressure wraps unless local emergency experts specifically direct them.
  • Spider bites: Clean gently, use a cool cloth or wrapped cool pack for comfort, and monitor symptoms. Seek medical advice for worsening pain, spreading redness, blistering, fever, muscle cramps, or uncertain/dangerous species; emergency care for systemic symptoms or breathing problems.
  • Scorpion stings: Wash with soap and water; apply a cool pack wrapped in cloth intermittently. Keep the person calm and seek poison-service/medical advice, particularly for children. Emergency assessment is needed for trouble breathing or swallowing, drooling, unusual eye movements, muscle twitching, severe agitation, or widespread symptoms. Do not cut, suck, or apply a tourniquet.

Aftercare: Watch for increasing redness, warmth, swelling, pus, fever, or worsening pain and seek medical care if these occur. Follow clinician/public-health advice on rabies vaccination, rabies immunoglobulin, tetanus boosters, and any prescribed antibiotics. Local protocols and species risks vary.

Do not: apply chemicals or traditional remedies, close a deep bite with glue, or ignore puncture wounds. Follow local emergency and public-health guidance.

Illustrated skills

How to apply a bandage

Simple visual steps for a head, arm, leg, and foot. These are general first-aid demonstrations—not a substitute for training or medical care.

Head bandage

  1. For a small scalp wound, place sterile gauze over it.
  2. Wrap a roller bandage around the head to hold the gauze, keeping it away from the eyes and ears.
  3. Secure gently; check that breathing and circulation remain normal.

Suspected skull injury, deep wound, heavy bleeding, or confusion: call emergency services. Do not press directly on a suspected skull fracture or embedded object.

Arm bandage

  1. Cover the wound with sterile dressing.
  2. Wrap from the area below the wound toward the body, overlapping each turn by about half the bandage width.
  3. Secure without pulling too tight; leave fingers visible and check warmth, color, feeling, and movement.

If bleeding is severe, use firm direct pressure and seek emergency help. A bandage should not cause numbness, tingling, or blue/cold fingers.

Leg bandage

  1. Cover the injury with a sterile pad.
  2. Wrap evenly around the leg, overlapping turns and supporting the dressing.
  3. Secure comfortably and check toes for normal color, warmth, sensation, and movement.

Do not wrap tightly over a suspected fracture or deformity. Keep the limb still and get medical help.

Foot bandage

  1. Place a sterile dressing over the wound.
  2. Wrap around the foot and ankle in overlapping turns, keeping the toes uncovered.
  3. Secure gently. Check toes for color, warmth, feeling, and movement; loosen if these worsen.

For severe pain, deformity, or inability to bear weight, keep the foot still and seek urgent care.

Bandage safety: If bleeding is life-threatening, call emergency services and apply firm direct pressure. Do not remove a soaked first dressing—add more on top. A bandage must not cut off circulation.
